# Prevention and Defense against Social Engineering Attacks

![Defending against social engineering](../resources/images/PreventionSocialEngineering.png)

*Image source: ChatGPT / DALL-E.*

Social engineering attacks exploit human psychology rather than technological vulnerabilities, which makes them hard to detect or prevent with technical controls alone. Defense is a mixture of awareness, written policy and regular testing.

The structure used here is the defensive framework from Christopher Hadnagy's book ["Social Engineering: The Science of Human Hacking"](https://www.amazon.com/Social-Engineering-Science-Human-Hacking/dp/111943338X) \[1\].

## Step 1: Learn to Identify Social Engineering Attacks

Recognize the forms a social engineering attack takes: phishing, pretexting and baiting. Awareness training teaches employees and individuals the signs and the tactics of these attacks.

## Step 2: Develop Actionable and Realistic Policies

Write policies for handling sensitive information and for the security protocols around it, clear enough that they can actually be enforced. Revisit them regularly as new threats and tactics appear.

## Step 3: Perform Regular Real-World Checkups

Thorough security audits identify vulnerabilities. Simulated attacks test whether the existing security measures hold.

## Step 4: Implement Applicable Security-Awareness Programs

Awareness programs run as continuous education, so that they keep up with evolving threats. Interactive training sessions hold attention better than passive ones.

## Creating a Security Awareness Culture

Cultivate a workplace culture in which security is everyone's responsibility. Real-world examples and past breaches are useful teaching material.

## Conclusion

Defense against social engineering spans awareness, policy, culture and continuous improvement. Hadnagy's M.A.P.P. (Mitigation and Prevention Plan) framework ties those together: learn to identify attacks, develop realistic policies, perform regular checkups, and run security-awareness programs.

No single measure prevents social engineering. The four steps above reduce the risk, and they are a starting point.
